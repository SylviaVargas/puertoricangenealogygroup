Civil registration in Puerto Rico did not begin until 1885. For the century and a half before that, the records that name ordinary people are church registers and a scattered, uneven body of municipal paperwork: household lists, labor registers, and population counts compiled by the *municipio* for its own administrative purposes.

Those municipal lists are the *padrones*. FamilySearch has now digitized hundreds of them, and they are almost entirely unknown to researchers because they are **not indexed by name**. Nothing in them will ever surface in a FamilySearch name search. You have to go find them.

> **What you can expect:** These are working administrative documents, not curated genealogical collections. Survival is uneven, handwriting is often difficult, and coverage varies enormously by municipality. What they offer in exchange is a name-level view of a population in decades where almost nothing else provides one.

---

## What a Padrón Is {#what-a-padron-is}

*Padrón* simply means a register or roll. In Puerto Rican practice it covers several different administrative exercises that share a common shape: an official walked a district and wrote down who lived there.

| Record type | Rough period | Created by | Names individuals? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Padrón de almas | 1700s to 1800s | Parish or municipality | Yes, by household |
| Padrón de jornaleros | 1849 to 1873 | Municipality | Yes, day laborers only |
| Censo poblacional municipal | 1800s to early 1900s | Municipality | Usually |
| Padrón de vecindad / cédulas | 1800s | Municipality | Yes, heads of household |

The distinction matters because each one was created for a different reason, and the reason determines who got left out. A *padrón de almas* aimed at everyone. A *padrón de jornaleros* deliberately covered only landless workers. Knowing which document you are reading tells you what an absence means.

## Padrón de Almas {#padron-de-almas}

A *padrón de almas*, literally a register of souls, is a household-by-household count of everyone living in a parish or municipality. The tradition comes out of Catholic parish practice, where the equivalent Latin term is *status animarum*, and it was adopted by civil authorities as well.

A typical entry gives:

- The head of household by name
- Spouse and children, usually with ages
- Other people in the house: relatives, servants, enslaved people, lodgers
- Sometimes race or status classification
- Sometimes the barrio or the property name

**Why it matters.** A padrón de almas is the closest thing Puerto Rico has to a household census before 1910. It captures a family as a unit at a moment in time, which lets you confirm sibling groups, catch a child who died before appearing in any other record, and identify the household members who will turn up later as marriage witnesses and baptism godparents.

> **Watch for the absences.** Enslaved people were sometimes listed by first name only, under the head of household rather than as a household of their own. Free people of color may appear in a separate section. Read the whole document structure before concluding someone is missing.

## Padrón de Jornaleros and the Régimen de la Libreta {#padron-de-jornaleros}

This is the record type most likely to be new to you, and for many families it is the only 19th-century document that names a landless ancestor.

In 1849 Governor Juan de la Pezuela issued the *Reglamento de Jornaleros*, which created what became known as the **Régimen de la Libreta**. Any man aged sixteen or older who did not own enough land to support himself was classified as a *jornalero*, a day laborer, and required to register with his municipality and carry a *libreta*, a passbook. The libreta recorded who he worked for, whether he had fulfilled his obligation, and any offenses. Being caught without one meant forced labor.

The system operated until 1873, the same year slavery was abolished in Puerto Rico. It is not a coincidence: both were mechanisms for compelling agricultural labor, and the libreta regime intensified as abolition approached.

**What the registers contain.** A padrón de jornaleros generally lists:

- Name of the worker
- Age
- Marital status
- Barrio of residence
- The hacienda or landowner who employed him
- Sometimes literacy, race, or place of birth

**Why it matters.** Landless working people are exactly the population that leaves the thinnest documentary trail. If your ancestor was not a landowner, did not appear in notarial records, and left only baptism and burial entries, the jornalero registers may be the one place that records his age, his barrio, and his employer in his own lifetime. The employer name is especially useful: it points you at a specific hacienda, and hacienda records and neighboring families become a FAN club to work with.

> **A note on reading these.** The libreta system was coercive, and the registers were compiled to control people, not to serve them. That does not make the information less accurate, but it does shape what the compiler chose to record and how. Read them as what they are.

## Censo Poblacional Municipal {#censo-poblacional-municipal}

Municipal population censuses sit between the parish padrón and the island-wide enumerations. They were compiled by the *municipio*, usually for tax, militia, or administrative planning, and their level of detail varies more than any other record type here.

Some are pure statistical summaries with no names at all. Others are full nominal schedules listing every resident. You cannot tell which you have until you open it, which is one more reason these collections reward browsing rather than searching.

For the island-wide censuses and their political context across the Spanish colonial, U.S. military, and U.S. federal periods, work through the [Census Records course](/research-guides/census-records/), particularly [Module 2 on Spanish colonial padrones](/research-guides/census-records-module-2/) and [Module 5 on municipal and barrio enumerations](/research-guides/census-records-module-5/).

## Reading an Image Group Title {#reading-an-image-group-title}

FamilySearch publishes these as **image groups**, not as indexed collections. Each group is a digitized archival box or bundle.

**Do not trust the title alone.** Nearly every one of these carries the same generic title:

```
Censo Poblacional
```

That tells you almost nothing. The information you actually need is in the **volume**, which FamilySearch displays beneath the title:

```
Caja 3, Censo de Almas
Caja 39, Padron de Habitantes
Caja 89, Cedulas Empadronamiento 3
Caja 71, Padrón de Jornaleros
```

Two pieces matter there:

- **The record type**, here *Censo de Almas* or *Padrón de Jornaleros*, tells you which of the document families above you are looking at. This is the single most useful thing on the page, and it is easy to miss because the title above it says something blander.
- **The *caja*** is the archival box number. It is the physical container the originals live in, and it is how the archive itself refers to the material. Record it in your research log: it is part of a complete citation, and it is what lets anyone else find the same document.

The place, the date range, and the image count come from the group's metadata. Our [Padrones Browser](/tools/padrones/) pulls all of these apart, reading the volume rather than the title, so you can filter on what the record actually is.

## Using the FamilySearch Image Search {#using-the-familysearch-image-search}

Most guidance on the internet, including older material on this site, tells you to browse the FamilySearch **Catalog** and click the camera icon. That still works. But FamilySearch also has a separate **Images** interface that searches the digitized images directly, and it exposes filters the Catalog does not.

The Images search lives at `familysearch.org/en/records/images/`. Two of its parameters do the heavy lifting for Puerto Rico research.

### Searching by creator {#searching-by-creator}

The `creator` parameter filters to the archival body that made the records. Nearly all of the material described in this guide was created by the *Gobierno de Puerto Rico*:

<a href="https://www.familysearch.org/en/records/images/search-results?creator=Gobierno%20de%20Puerto%20Rico&pageSize=100" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><em>All Gobierno de Puerto Rico image groups</em></a>

As of August 2026 that search returns roughly 900 image groups. Around 170 of those are island-wide teacher personnel files rather than padrones, which leaves **729 municipal records across 20 municipalities**. Those 729 are what our [Padrones Browser](/tools/padrones/) indexes.

Coverage is concentrated rather than even: Arecibo alone accounts for 192 image groups and Isabela for 185, while most of the 78 municipalities have none at all. That is survival, not selection. Municipal paper was lost to hurricanes, fire, insects, and neglect at very different rates from town to town.

### Filtering by record type {#filtering-by-record-type}

The `recordTypeIds` parameter narrows to a category. Three of them cover most of what is useful here:

| Record type | Parameter | Approximate count | Saved search |
|---|---|---|---|
| Censo Poblacional / Municipal Census | `recordTypeIds=130138` | 515 | <a href="https://www.familysearch.org/en/records/images/search-results?creator=Gobierno%20de%20Puerto%20Rico&recordTypeIds=130138&pageSize=100" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><em>Open</em></a> |
| Inhabitant Lists, including jornaleros and almas | `recordTypeIds=126590` | 117 | <a href="https://www.familysearch.org/en/records/images/search-results?creator=Gobierno%20de%20Puerto%20Rico&recordTypeIds=126590&pageSize=100" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><em>Open</em></a> |
| Enslavement Records | `recordTypeIds=126864` | 58, mostly Arecibo | <a href="https://www.familysearch.org/en/records/images/search-results?creator=Gobierno%20de%20Puerto%20Rico&recordTypeIds=126864&pageSize=100" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><em>Open</em></a> |

You can also raise `pageSize` to 100 to see more results per page, which makes scanning far faster than the default.

> **A free FamilySearch account is required** to view the images themselves. The search results are visible without one, but the viewer is not.

For the enslavement records specifically, read them alongside the 1872 *Registro Central de Esclavos* covered in [Module 2 of the Slave Records course](/research-guides/slave-records-module-2/). The two document different moments and different administrative purposes.

## Browsing Without a Name Index {#browsing-without-a-name-index}

There is no shortcut around the fact that these images are unindexed. There is, however, a method that keeps the work bounded.

1. **Fix the municipality first.** Everything here is organized by *municipio*. If you are not certain which town your family was in, resolve that before you open a single image. The [Municipality Guide](/tools/municipality-guide/) exists for this.
2. **Fix the date range second.** Pick the tightest window your other evidence supports. A padrón is a snapshot; you want the one taken while your family was actually there.
3. **Open the group and look for structure.** Most groups begin with a cover page or an index, and many are organized by barrio. Use the thumbnail view to find those divider pages rather than scrolling image by image.
4. **Work by barrio, not alphabetically.** These lists were compiled by walking a route. Households near each other on the page were near each other on the ground. That is a feature: the neighbors you find are your FAN club.
5. **Record what you searched, including the empty results.** A padrón that covers your family's barrio in the right year and does *not* list them is meaningful. See [Module 4 on research logs](/research-guides/getting-started-module-4/).

> **Key principle:** A failed name search does not mean the record does not exist. In this material, there was never a name search to fail.

## Evidence Analysis {#evidence-analysis}

Apply the same framework you would to any source.

**Source type.** A padrón viewed as a digital image on FamilySearch is a **derivative** of an **original** source. The original is the manuscript register in the archive, identified by its *caja* number. The image is a faithful copy, but your citation should make clear which one you examined.

**Information quality.** Mostly **primary**: the compiling official generally recorded what he observed or was told at the household. But ages are frequently **secondary** and frequently wrong, since they were reported by whoever answered the door. Treat an age in a padrón as an approximation.

**Evidence value.** A padrón is **direct evidence** of residence at a place and time. It is usually **indirect evidence** of relationships, since it records who was in a house rather than stating how they were related. And where a family that should appear does not, it is **negative evidence** worth documenting.

**Conflicts.** When a padrón age conflicts with a baptism record, the baptism record generally wins: it was created closer to the event by an informant with better knowledge. Do not average them. Reason about which informant was in a position to know.

## Citing These Records {#citing-these-records}

A complete citation needs the archival identifiers, not just the URL. Following Evidence Explained conventions:

> "Padrón de jornaleros, caja 12," 1866–1873, Arecibo, Puerto Rico; imaged as "Puerto Rico, registros municipales," image group, FamilySearch (https://www.familysearch.org/ : accessed 14 August 2026); citing Gobierno de Puerto Rico municipal records.

Include the *caja* number, the municipality, the date range, the image number within the group, and your access date.
